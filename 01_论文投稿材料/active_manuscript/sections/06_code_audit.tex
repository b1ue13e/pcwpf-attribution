\section{Software Implementation Fidelity: The Physics-Constrained Transformer}
\label{sec:code_audit}

Verifying physical constraint claims requires examining not only published mathematical equations, but also the computational fidelity of the source code executed during training and evaluation.

\subsection{Code Availability and Audit Scope}
As established in our scoping audit (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), exactly two of the 46 scoping studies provided accessible software repositories: PINN-UQ \cite{s10_pinnuq2025}, which we audited and verified as code-aligned with its published electromechanical formulations ($\mathcal{D}_4 = \texttt{code-aligned}$), and the \textbf{Physics-Constrained Transformer (PCT)} \cite{pct2026} (\textit{Scientific Reports} 2026, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-34348-x; official GitHub repository commit \texttt{65941ec}). PCT represents the sole publicly accessible training codebase for end-to-end physics-constrained Transformers and the sole audited repository exhibiting an implementation divergence between published equations and computational execution ($\mathcal{D}_4 = \texttt{code-mismatch}$). We conducted an in-depth static and empirical audit of this codebase to investigate how statistical distribution constraints are realized in software.

\subsection{Mathematical Specification versus Computational Execution}
In Section 2 of the PCT paper, the authors propose regularizing a Transformer forecaster by penalizing statistical divergence between the empirical joint distribution of historical wind speed and turbine power ($P_{\text{ref}}$) and the joint distribution predicted by the model ($P_{\text{pred}}$). The paper explicitly specifies the symmetric \textbf{Jensen--Shannon (JS) divergence}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}}(P_{\text{pred}} \parallel P_{\text{ref}}) &= \frac{1}{2} \text{KL}\left(P_{\text{pred}} \,\Big\|\, \frac{P_{\text{pred}} + P_{\text{ref}}}{2}\right) \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{2} \text{KL}\left(P_{\text{ref}} \,\Big\|\, \frac{P_{\text{pred}} + P_{\text{ref}}}{2}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:js_divergence}
\end{equation}
where $\text{KL}(p \parallel q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i}$ denotes standard Kullback--Leibler divergence. The paper notes that the symmetry and boundedness of JS divergence ($0 \le \mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}} \le \ln 2$) provide numerical stability.

However, static AST inspection of the official training script (\texttt{train\_clean.py}, lines 349--352; commit \texttt{65941ec}) traces the loss execution to:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{minipage}{0.95\columnwidth}
\texttt{loss\_phy = 0.5 * (} \\
\hspace*{1em}\texttt{F.kl\_div(pred\_kde.log(), m\_kde, ...)} \\
\hspace*{1em}\texttt{+ F.kl\_div(true\_kde.log(), m\_kde, ...))}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
In the PyTorch C++ ATen kernel backend (\texttt{native/Loss.cpp}, implementing \texttt{kl\_div\_backward\_cpu}), the functional specification for \texttt{torch.nn.functional.kl\_div(input, target)} evaluates:
\begin{equation}
\text{\texttt{kl\_div}}(\text{input}, \text{target}) = \sum_{i} \text{target}_i \cdot \big( \log \text{target}_i - \text{input}_i \big).
\label{eq:pytorch_kl}
\end{equation}
Passing \texttt{input} as $\log p$ and \texttt{target} as $q$ binds the tensor arguments such that the C++ kernel computes:
\begin{equation}
\text{\texttt{kl\_div}}(\log p, q) = \sum_{i} q_i \cdot \big( \log q_i - \log p_i \big) = \text{KL}(q \parallel p).
\label{eq:reverse_kl}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the computational graph generated in \texttt{train\_clean.py} executes:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{code}} &= \frac{1}{2} \text{KL}\left(M \parallel P_{\text{pred}}\right) + \frac{1}{2} \text{KL}\left(M \parallel P_{\text{ref}}\right), \\
\text{where } M &= \frac{P_{\text{pred}} + P_{\text{ref}}}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reverse_js}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reverse_js} establishes an altered objective, which we designate the \textbf{reverse-argument JS form}. The formulation remains symmetric between $P_{\text{pred}}$ and $P_{\text{ref}}$. However, transposing the KL arguments alters the underlying gradient mechanics. Because the mixture distribution $M = \frac{1}{2}(P_{\text{pred}} + P_{\text{ref}})$ explicitly depends on $P_{\text{pred}}$ with $\frac{\partial M_i}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} = \frac{1}{2}$, differentiating $\mathcal{L}_{\text{code}}$ directly with respect to predicted probability mass $P_{\text{pred}, i}$ yields:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{code}}}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} &= \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} \left[ M_i \log \frac{M_i}{P_{\text{pred}, i}} \right] \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} \left[ M_i \log \frac{M_i}{P_{\text{ref}, i}} \right] \\
&= \frac{1}{4} \left[ \log \left(\frac{M_i^2}{P_{\text{pred}, i} P_{\text{ref}, i}}\right) + 1 - \frac{P_{\text{ref}, i}}{P_{\text{pred}, i}} \right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reverse_js_gradient}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reverse_js_gradient} exposes an analytical \textbf{inverse-density singularity}: whenever the model assigns near-zero probability to a region where empirical training observations exist ($P_{\text{pred}, i} \to 0$ while $P_{\text{ref}, i} > 0$), the ratio $\frac{P_{\text{ref}, i}}{P_{\text{pred}, i}}$ diverges. Even under machine-precision clamping ($P_{\text{pred}, i} \ge \epsilon$), this term scales as $\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)$, generating steep negative gradient responses.

In contrast, for the standard mathematically specified Jensen--Shannon divergence $\mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:js_divergence}, differentiating with respect to $P_{\text{pred}, i}$ gives:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}}}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} = \frac{1}{2} \log \left(\frac{P_{\text{pred}, i}}{M_i}\right).
\label{eq:standard_js_gradient}
\end{equation}
Because $M_i = \frac{1}{2}(P_{\text{pred}, i} + P_{\text{ref}, i}) \ge \frac{1}{2}P_{\text{pred}, i}$, the ratio $\frac{P_{\text{pred}, i}}{M_i} \in [0, 2]$. Consequently, the gradient of Standard-JS is strictly upper-bounded:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}}}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} \le \frac{1}{2} \ln 2 \approx 0.3466.
\end{equation}
As predicted probabilities approach zero ($P_{\text{pred}, i} \to \epsilon$ with $P_{\text{ref}, i} > 0$), the gradient of Standard-JS exhibits only a mild, integrable \textbf{logarithmic divergence}:
\begin{equation}
\left| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{JS}}}{\partial P_{\text{pred}, i}} \right| \sim \mathcal{O}(|\log \epsilon|).
\end{equation}
Because the logarithmic divergence of Standard-JS grows slowly ($\lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \epsilon \log \epsilon = 0$) and possesses a finite upper bound ($\frac{1}{2}\ln 2$), this divergence is substantially milder than the $\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)$ inverse-density pole of the reverse-argument JS form, consistent with the lower gradient norms observed empirically in Figure~\ref{fig:pct_contrast}. Transposing divergence arguments thus alters optimization dynamics near empirical support boundaries.

\subsection{Empirical Optimization Contrast: Official Reverse-JS vs.\ Corrected Standard-JS}
To determine whether this implementation divergence materially alters neural training and operational dispatch, we trained models across Kelmarsh and Penmanshiel under five random seeds across $\lambda \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$, benchmarked against unconstrained baselines (Figure~\ref{fig:pct_contrast}). The empirical results reveal two distinct operational effects:

First, the reverse-argument JS form induces marked gradient amplification: at $\lambda = 1.0$, it produces an average gradient $L_2$ norm of $0.1665 \pm 0.0160$ on Kelmarsh and $0.2465 \pm 0.0217$ on Penmanshiel, representing a $2.20\times$ and $2.72\times$ amplification over Corrected Standard-JS ($0.0756 \pm 0.0048$ on Kelmarsh and $0.0907 \pm 0.0067$ on Penmanshiel; Figure~\ref{fig:pct_contrast}a). This elevation reflects the $-\frac{P_{\text{ref}, i}}{4 P_{\text{pred}, i}}$ derivative term as predicted densities approach boundary limits. Second, at weak regularization ($\lambda = 0.01$ on Penmanshiel), Corrected Standard-JS achieves a dispatch cost of $0.09804 \pm 0.00024$ (outperforming the unconstrained baseline of $0.09864$), whereas the official reverse-argument implementation inflates dispatch cost to $0.09997 \pm 0.00086$ (a $+1.35\%$ degradation; Figure~\ref{fig:pct_contrast}b). However, at strong regularization ($\lambda=1.0$), both divergence variants degrade performance relative to the unconstrained baseline, with Corrected Standard-JS reaching $0.10549 \pm 0.00149$ compared to $0.10292 \pm 0.00103$ for the reverse form. Correcting the implementation divergence mitigates gradient amplification and eliminates premature degradation under weak penalties, but does not prevent performance loss under excessive regularization.

\subsection{Negative Control: Algebraic Verification of 2D Product-Kernel KDE}
We establish an algebraic negative control to verify whether tensor outer-product operations preserve empirical feature coupling in the audited codebase. A potential concern regarding the tensor outer-product:
\begin{equation}
G = K_p^\top K_w \in \mathbb{R}^{B_p \times B_w}
\label{eq:outer_product}
\end{equation}
is that it might assume statistical independence between wind speed and turbine power ($p(w, P) = p(w)p(P)$).

Formal algebraic expansion refutes this concern. Let $\{ (P_s, w_s) \}_{s=1}^N$ denote paired empirical observations in a batch. Let $\{b_i^p\}_{i=1}^{B_p}$ and $\{b_j^w\}_{j=1}^{B_w}$ denote 1D evaluation bin centers for power and wind speed, respectively. The 1D Gaussian kernel vectors evaluated in the codebase are:
\begin{equation}
\left[K_p\right]_{s,i} = \phi\left( \frac{b_i^p - P_s}{h_p} \right), \quad \left[K_w\right]_{s,j} = \phi\left( \frac{b_j^w - w_s}{h_w} \right),
\label{eq:kernel_eval}
\end{equation}
where $\phi(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-u^2/2}$ is the standard Gaussian kernel, and $h_p, h_w$ denote bandwidths. Expanding the matrix product element at grid coordinate $(i,j)$ yields:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left[ K_p^\top K_w \right]_{i,j} &= \sum_{s=1}^N \left[K_p\right]_{s,i} \cdot \left[K_w\right]_{s,j} \\
&= \sum_{s=1}^N \phi\left( \frac{b_i^p - P_s}{h_p} \right) \cdot \phi\left( \frac{b_j^w - w_s}{h_w} \right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kde_proof}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kde_proof} is the exact analytical definition of a standard \textbf{paired 2D product-kernel Kernel Density Estimator} on correlated bivariate data. Because the summation contracts over concurrent sample index $s$, empirical joint coupling between $P_s$ and $w_s$ is preserved. The algebraic expansion confirms that the outer product executes authentic bivariate density estimation, confirming that code discrepancies are localized to divergence argument order rather than upstream kernel construction.
